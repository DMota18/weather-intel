The problem

Concrete is unforgiving. Below 40°F the hydration reaction stalls and it never reaches strength. Above 95°F it flash-sets and cracks. Rain on a fresh pour washes out the surface. Wind dries the top faster than the bottom and it curls. Sealer needs a dry slab for 24 hours before and 24 hours after — and it cures overnight, so a warm afternoon means nothing if it drops to 35°F at 2am.

A normal weather app says "72°F, partly cloudy." That doesn't answer the question a crew needs answered at 6am. Getting it wrong costs thousands of dollars — a ruined pour means a tear-out and a redo.

What it does

It scores every hour of a {{ forecast_days }}-day forecast against concrete-specific thresholds — temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation probability, and dew point spread — and rolls those hours into a green / yellow / red verdict for the 7am–5pm working day, with the best usable window and a plain-English reason.

Three separate decisions, because they have different physics: pour (hour-by-hour conditions), seal (dry 24h behind and ahead, plus the overnight low), and cure (freeze, rain, heat, and wind across the 48 hours after a pour). National Weather Service severe-weather alerts surface on the dashboard.

The engineering decision that matters most

Real money rides on a green verdict, which makes this a system where the failure modes are asymmetric: a wrong "red" costs a day of scheduling, a wrong "green" costs a tear-out. So the whole scoring engine is built on one rule — missing data must never improve a verdict.

A bug this rule was written to kill

The day verdict was built as "collect the scored working hours, then call it green unless something is red or yellow." When that list came back empty — no data, or a forecast fetched in the evening after the working day had passed — the day was labeled green. Every evening, "today" showed GO regardless of actual conditions.

How it works now

An hour with any missing input carries a data factor that caps it at yellow, so partial data can never reach green. A day with no scorable working hours returns no data and renders as a gray NO DATA chip. When a source is unreachable, the API serves the last known good reading explicitly flagged stale, and returns a 503 that says treat as no-go rather than guessing. Unknown is never safe.

Engineering notes

  • Parsing NOAA GHCN-Daily

    Fixed-width records, one line per station-month-element, 31 day slots each with value + 3 flags. Missing is -9999, temperatures are in tenths of °C, precipitation in tenths of mm. Values failing NOAA's quality flags are dropped rather than trusted, and short months are handled by calendar rather than by guessing.

  • Time is local, always

    The 7am–5pm working window is meaningless in UTC. Forecasts are requested in America/New_York and every window — last 24h, next 24h, the working day — is sliced by real timestamps rather than list position, which is what a naive slice gets wrong when a response starts at midnight instead of "now."

  • Degradation is a feature

    Upstream calls retry with backoff, then fall back to last-known-good data marked stale, then fail loudly. The app boots and serves verdicts with PostgreSQL and Redis both down, because the pour decision doesn't need either.

  • Tested where it counts

    {% if test_count %}{{ test_count }} tests{% else %}The test suite{% endif %} concentrate on the scoring engine and its edge cases — threshold boundaries, zero-degree readings that must not read as "missing," missing-data caps, and the working-window boundary. CI runs tests, Ruff, and SQLFluff against a live PostgreSQL on every push, then deploys with a health check and automatic rollback.
